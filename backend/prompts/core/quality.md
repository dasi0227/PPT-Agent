## Direction and Restraint

- Choose a visual direction suited to the subject, audience, and presentation setting, grounded in the user's requirements.
- Maintain a coherent visual language across the deck, including typography, shapes, borders, corner treatments, and shadows. Treat equivalent content consistently.
- Give each slide a clear focal point and supporting hierarchy. Concentrate the strongest visual treatment on what the audience should notice first.
- Aim for a refined, simple, and orderly visual style. Avoid excessive layering, flashy effects, overly complex compositions, and visual clutter so the content remains the focus.

## Color and Emphasis

- Give equally important items consistent backgrounds, borders, and text treatments. Reserve stronger emphasis for an item that is explicitly the focus or has a clear difference in status or priority.
- Emphasize essential words, phrases, and conclusions with selective bolding, an emphasis color, or a modest increase in size. Keep surrounding content quieter so the emphasis remains distinct.
- Keep text, icons, and diagram elements clearly readable against their backgrounds. Avoid color combinations that make important content difficult to distinguish.

## Typography and Wording

- Give each slide a concise, prominent title that is visibly larger than body text. Keep title scale and placement consistent across comparable slides.
- Use a clear type hierarchy for headings, body text, labels, and captions. Keep equivalent roles consistent, and tune line length, line height, and wrapping to the language and available space.
- Use precise phrases and short paragraphs, often two or three concise sentences. Keep each text block focused on one point and use parallel phrasing for peer items.
- Use concise, informative wording with a professional presentation tone. State findings, reasons, and implications directly; remove filler, slogans, stock phrases, and irrelevant detail.
- Use plain, direct language and explain necessary technical terms at the audience's level. Avoid roundabout phrasing, lengthy exposition, and jargon that adds no useful precision.
- Use relatable analogies and concrete examples to make unfamiliar ideas easier to understand or remember. Keep comparisons accurate and appropriate to the audience and occasion.
- Use light, relevant humor when it helps sustain attention and makes the presentation more engaging. Keep it natural and restrained, avoiding forced jokes, childish phrasing, or distractions from the main point.

## Layout and Alignment

- Establish an evident reading order from the main message to its supporting material. Place related content close together and give distinct groups clear separation.
- Align related elements along shared edges, baselines, or centers. Align corresponding regions inside peer blocks, including headings, body text, visuals, and summaries.
- Plan enough space for varying text lengths and line counts in each region. Longer content must not squeeze neighboring regions or push later content out of alignment with its counterparts in peer blocks.
- Use consistent padding, margins, gaps, line spacing, and paragraph spacing for equivalent elements and text roles.
- Choose a composition that expresses the content relationship, such as a split layout, timeline, stepped sequence, or radial arrangement. Keep alignment and reading order clear in both grid and non-grid compositions.

## Visuals and Shapes

- Support explanations with relevant examples, images, diagrams, charts, or code, sized for clear viewing at presentation scale. 
- Use unordered lists for peer points and ordered lists for steps, sequences, or explicit rankings. Keep list items concise and structurally consistent.
- Use tables to compare multiple objects across shared attributes. Keep row and column labels clear and corresponding values easy to compare.
- Use charts to show quantitative comparisons, trends, or composition. Keep proportions and scales accurate, label values and units clearly, and identify illustrative data as examples.
- Use diagrams to explain structures, processes, and relationships. Give positions, grouping, connectors, and arrows clear meanings that support the intended reading order.
- Use icons to represent concepts and make content easier to scan. Keep related icons consistent in style, stroke weight, and visual size, and retain text labels when an icon's meaning is not immediately clear.
- Use cards and familiar presentation shapes as text containers, including rectangles, circles, ellipses, and polygons. Choose shapes that suit the content and its relationships, and leave enough space for readable text.

## Density and Whitespace

- Match information density to the slide's role and the audience's needs. Make each region carry useful content while remaining easy to scan and understand.
- Leave comfortable margins around essential content on all four sides of the canvas. Keep body text, captions, chart labels, and table details readable at presentation scale while preventing unintended overlap or clipping.
- Use whitespace to separate groups, frame a focal point, and balance the composition. Keep related content compact without packing every available space or creating large empty areas without a compositional purpose.

## Narrative and Rhythm

- Build a smooth narrative with clear logic and an easy-to-follow progression. Keep each slide's main point clear and connect it naturally to the next.
- Use transition slides when moving between major topics or phases. Briefly introduce what comes next and clarify its connection to the preceding section, keeping these slides within the agreed slide count.
- Include an agenda when the presentation's length or complexity calls for an overview. Show the main sections in presentation order using concise, informative labels.
- Maintain a consistent tone, writing style, and terminology throughout the deck. Keep wording and sentence patterns coherent across slides so the presentation reads as a cohesive whole.
